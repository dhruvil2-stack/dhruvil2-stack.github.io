Each answer needed the other

Build log · ARVPOctober 2026MATLAB · BVD modelling

Our underwater transducer has two resonances on top of each other. To measure the weak one I had to remove the strong one, but the strong one’s numbers were bent by the weak one. Here’s how I peeled them apart.

What I was modelling

The ARVP RoboSub team is building an FSK acoustic modem from potted piezoelectric transducers. To design the transmit driver in LTspice we need a circuit that behaves like the transducer, so the simulation sees the right load.

On October 17 we swept the impedance of each transducer underwater from 20 to 200 kHz, three times each, and I averaged the runs point by point. The standard circuit for this is the Butterworth–Van Dyke (BVD) model: a capacitor C0 for the ceramic itself, in parallel with a series R–L–C branch that stands in for the mechanical vibration (Van Dyke, 1928; IEEE Std 176-1987).

One branch wasn’t enough

The single-branch values come straight off the sweep. In admittance, Y = 1/Z, parallel parts simply add, so I worked there. The conductance G = Re(Y) peaks at the series resonance, fs = 151.7 kHz, and at that frequency L and C cancel, leaving only R. The resistance Rs peaks at the anti-resonance, fp = 174.2 kHz.

R1 = 1 / G(fs),   C0 = B(fs) / 2πfs C1 = C0 [ (fp/fs)² − 1 ],   L1 = 1 / (2πfs)² C1 B = Im(Y) is the susceptance. At fs the branch is purely resistive, so all of B there belongs to C0.

That gave C0 = 4.46 nF, R1 = 122 Ω and Q ≈ 6 for the main mode. The model fits the resonance well, but below about 60 kHz it sits far above the measurement: 18.0% RMS error over the sweep and up to 29% near 40 kHz. That’s exactly where our carrier band is, 45 to 65 kHz, so it mattered.

The catch

The extra error is a second, weaker vibration mode. Transducers with more than one resonance need one R–L–C branch per mode (Sherman and Butler, 2007, ch. 13). To find the second branch, the obvious move is to subtract C0 and branch 1 from the measured admittance and look at what’s left.

The problem is that C0 and R1 were read at 151.7 kHz, and the second branch is still contributing a little conductance and susceptance there. So C0 and branch 1 are slightly wrong until branch 2 is known, and branch 2 can’t be measured until C0 and branch 1 are right. Each one needs the other first.

Peeling, twice

The way around it is to guess, then correct. My script runs the same loop two times.

  1. Subtract what you have. Using the current C0 and branch 1, compute what’s left over:
    Yleft = Y − jωC0 − 1 / Z1Z1 = R1 + jωL1 + 1/jωC1, the branch 1 impedance at every frequency.
  2. Find the hump. The leftover conductance has a broad peak. I only search 30 to 100 kHz, because residue from the main mode piles up near 150 kHz. The peak sits at f2 = 64.7 kHz, and R2 = 1/Gleft,max = 1816 Ω.
  3. Get its Q from one side. The high side of the hump runs into branch 1, so I only use the low side: step left from the peak to half height, interpolate to land on it exactly, and use
    Q2 = 1 / ( f2/flo − flo/f2 ) ≈ 1.06L2 = Q2R2 / 2πf2,   C2 = 1 / 2πf2Q2R2
  4. Take branch 2 back out of the first readings. Now that it’s known, remove its share at fs and redo branch 1:
    C0 = [ B(fs) − Im Y2(fs) ] / 2πfsR1 = 1 / [ G(fs) − Re Y2(fs) ]
    then recompute C1 and L1 with the new C0. On the second pass the loop starts from these corrected values.

Two passes were enough: the second barely moved the numbers. The correction took C0 from 4.46 to 4.69 nF and R1 from 121.7 to 123.4 Ω.

The RMS error over the whole sweep went from 18.0% to 3.0%, and inside the 45–65 kHz carrier band from 17.2% to 1.1%. Afterwards I added a least-squares refinement with MATLAB’s fminsearch, a Nelder–Mead search (Lagarias et al., 1998), started from the peeled values. It brought the overall error to 1.7%, and it was needed on Transducer 2, where the coarse sweep grid had missed the true peak.

The second mode is broad, with a Q near 1, and it sits right on our carrier band. That’s useful: it means the transducer can cover 45–65 kHz without smearing bits, and the model now gives the transmit driver the right load there.

Checking it in the pool

The model says the band around 45–65 kHz is usable. The same day we ran a gated-pulse sweep from 30 to 80 kHz at 20, 40 and 46 inches. The detections cluster between about 40 and 70 kHz, which agrees, though we’re still validating this data before using it for decisions.
